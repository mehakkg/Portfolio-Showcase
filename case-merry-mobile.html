01 · THE BUSINESS

A B2B product whose revenue depends on hospitals actually using it.

Merry Health connects Tier-2 and Tier-3 private hospitals (200+ beds) with ambulance partners. It sells to hospital heads of sales and marketing: faster pickups from smaller-town catchments mean more admissions.

~30hospitals live
50–60%of rides were hospital-to-hospital transfers
WHY ADOPTION IS REVENUE
₹3,000per month · failed, hospitals are price-sensitive→₹200per ride · revenue scales with every request

Every request that skips the system is revenue that disappears.

THREE BUSINESS GOALS
01Grow revenue per hospital through rides booked on-platform
02Collect clean, timestamped data for reporting, billing and SLAs
03Sign up more hospitals by proving reliability

02 · THE GAP

The workflow on paper and on the ground were two different systems.

WHY IT HAPPENED
  • →Emergency staff are overstretched, on the move and rarely at a desk
  • →Many aren’t comfortable with digital tools. WhatsApp needs no learning
  • →No regulation requires digital tracking, so the dashboard was a nice-to-have
WHAT IT COST
  • →The ops team became a human integration layer, 24×7
  • →Timestamps, and the reports and invoices built on them, were only as good as manual entry
  • →Hospitals saw the dashboard as a month-end report, not a live tool
Two incidents that shaped the data model
01A 130 kg patient on a third floor with no lift and narrow stairs. It needed extra attendants and a chair lift.Lift, floor and stretcher-fit became booking fields.
02An ambulance dispatched without oxygen after a verbal miscommunication.Equipment became an explicit selection, never a phone-call detail.

03 · WHO DECIDES ADOPTION

Ten actors touch every ride. One decides if it gets used.

I mapped every stakeholder by how directly they affect adoption, dispatch speed and data quality. Hospital admins came out as the lever: they start every request, so their channel choice decides whether a ride is recorded, billed and tracked at all.

Hospital admins start every request. So I designed for them first.

04 · HOW I WORKED

Understand the decisions before designing the screens.

Every step produced an artifact the next one depended on. Research came first: an emergency workflow you don’t understand fails in ways no usability test catches.

  1. 01Admin decision mapEvery decision an admin makes, the information it needs, the channel, who they talk to and what “done” means.
  2. 02Data auditEvery data point cut to the minimum needed to dispatch: phone number, pickup pin, ambulance type. The rest became conditional or deferred.
  3. 03Scenario & journey mappingA patient collapses at home in a Tier-2 city. I traced the admin’s 8-step flow, then mapped the ideal journey.
  4. 04Phase-wise system auditIssues in each dispatch phase, scored by probability × impact and checked against Nielsen’s heuristics.
  5. 05Workflow, DPs & DOsA screen-level workflow for live rides, with error states, design problems and opportunities per screen.
  6. 066-Ups ideation → hi-fiSix concepts per screen, one hybrid chosen, then specs, a hi-fi prototype and the client walkthrough.

05 · THE AUDIT

Three of five phases were critical. None were UI problems.

I set one rule for recommendations: no interface words. Each had to name the failing workflow step, how to restructure it, and the expected impact.

06 · STRATEGY

Adoption before automation.

The first idea was AI-assisted dispatch: record the call, turn speech into text, auto-fill and auto-assign. It broke in the vision call.

WHY AI DISPATCH BROKE
  • →It needed phone-system integration
  • →Low-confidence fields pulled humans back in
  • →Drivers didn’t trust automated calls
  • →A voice note can’t give a pickup pin
So the product had to absorb behaviour before replacing it. AI moved to the roadmap, behind a gate.

Three decisions, signed off by Vedang.

1WhatsApp stays the front doorRequests and status broadcasts flow through the channel admins already use. Fighting it had already cost the product its adoption.
2The dashboard becomes the recordEvery update, from any channel, lands in one timestamped source of truth for tracking, reporting, billing and compliance.
3A manual fallback in every critical journeyAmbulances are life-critical. Every automated step has a human path for when GPS, network or a driver fails.

07 · WORKFLOW & DECISIONS

Designing for the worst hour: five rides live at once.

I built the workflow around the hardest real scenario: parallel emergencies, updates scattered across chats and calls, and everyone asking “where is the ambulance?”

Six concepts per screen, one hybrid, the trade-off stated.

2.0 · ACTIVE RIDESA priority list that sorts itself.Rides cluster into Critical, At Risk and On Track. No update for several minutes, or a slipping ETA, moves a ride up. It works the same at 3 rides or 50.TRADE-OFFOverrides chronological order and needs thresholds tuned with the ops team.
2.1 · RIDE DETAILSRide health in two seconds.A health summary on top (status, ETA, driver state, halts), then the live map and event timeline, with alerts that suggest the next action.TRADE-OFFDepends on reliable GPS and needs firm visual hierarchy to avoid density.
2.1.1 · MAP VIEWPlanned vs actual route.Deviations are highlighted, with labels like “No movement 3+ min” or “Off-route”, and a mini timeline so context isn’t lost while zoomed in.TRADE-OFFHigher engineering effort for route and traffic data.
2.2 · DELAY ALERTRoot cause first, then the action.The panel says why (a halt, a network drop, an ETA jump), puts three actions up front, and codes severity so minor delays stay quiet.TRADE-OFFFalse alerts erode trust, so thresholds must be conservative.
3.0 · HANDOVERIts own step, not a field on a page.Tracking is for watching, handover is for verifying. A dedicated panel runs geofence arrival with a checklist fallback.TRADE-OFFOne extra step, kept because it creates the timestamp billing depends on.
4.0 · TRIP CLOSUREThe system checks before the human signs.Auto-validation flags missing timestamps, conflicting arrival data and bulk-closure patterns. A trip can’t close until its record is complete.TRADE-OFFRequires standardised status events from the driver side.

08 · THE EXPERIENCE

Built for an admin with a phone in one hand.

Five screens, built from the decisions above.

01 · DASHBOARDA command centre, not a monthly report
  • →Notifications for delays, arrivals and unresponsive drivers
  • →Live map of everything heading to the hospital
  • →Case severity trends for staffing and peak hours
02 · ADD RIDEDispatch on the minimum, complete the rest later
  • →Map-based pickup and drop, no long address typing
  • →Emergency short form with only the essential fields
  • →Equipment and facilities as explicit selections
  • →Missing-field indicators instead of blocking validation
03 · RIDE DETAILSAnswers “where is it?” without a phone call
  • →Trip timeline and live vehicle map together
  • →Status snapshot: last update, distance left, speed
  • →Call driver and escalate when the system can’t resolve it
04 · MARK HANDOVERAn audit trail by design
  • →Hospital → driver → admin confirmations, in order
  • →Detected hospital, arrival time and GPS confidence shown
  • →Close is enabled only when every check passes
05 · ANALYTICS & REPORTSProof of value for hospital leadership
  • →KPI cards for trips, billing, response time and turnaround
  • →Filters by date, case type, status and ambulance type
  • →PDF, CSV and Excel exports for month-end reviews

09 · OUTCOME

An agreed roadmap, and a measurable definition of success.

The engagement ended with a 20-minute strategy pitch and a walkthrough of the hi-fi prototype. Because the real problem was adoption, not “a better dashboard”, I aligned the client on four KPIs:

01 · ADOPTIONRequests raised through the dashboard or WhatsApp automationBaseline ~5% · gate ≥70%
02 · OPERATIONSAverage dispatch response time, request to pickupOn time = under 20 min
03 · RELIABILITYTracking link delivery success to patient familiesEn route rated Critical
04 · ENGAGEMENTWeekly active hospitals across dashboard and WhatsAppStarting base ~30 hospitals
BEFOREChats, calls, paper notes, and a dashboard filled in after the fact.
DESIGNED FOROne record fed by the channel admins already use, with push updates and verified closure.

10 · CONSTRAINTS & REFLECTION

What I worked within, and what I’d keep.

CONSTRAINT ↳ HOW I HANDLED IT
Four weeks from brief to handover↳Used severity scores to decide what not to design
Client inputs (process docs, roadmap) arrived late↳Used workflow maps as the shared language with client and team
Drivers on feature phones; GPS and network unreliable↳Designed a fallback for every automated step
Price-sensitive buyers: nothing could add cost or training↳Wrote recommendations engineers could size, not mockups to admire
REFLECTIONThe time spent understanding the system early was the time we saved later. The most useful thing I designed wasn’t a screen. It was the decision about what to automate, and when.
01Fixing individual screens wouldn’t have moved adoption. Changing the workflow could.
02In emergency software, the product’s real competitor is WhatsApp, and you can’t out-feature it. You have to integrate with it.
03A team moves fastest when each person owns a slice end to end. Mine was the hospital admin.
