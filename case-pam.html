§ 01  ·  The Problem

miniOrange's PAM had everything a buyer's checklist asks for. Nobody could use it.

miniOrange's Privileged Access Management product had everything a PAM buyer's checklist asks for. Vaulting, session recording, access policies, discovery, MFA. On a feature comparison spreadsheet, it looked competitive.

Every admin who tried to actually use it hit the same wall.

The product was 14 disconnected nav items masquerading as an ecosystem. Setting up a single privileged workflow required jumping between three or four unrelated sections. Object names contradicted what admins actually meant. "System Users" was where credentials lived, not users. Errors happened silently. Discovery surfaced data but didn't guide action. Critical enterprise capabilities like JIT access, emergency break glass, session OCR, reconciliation were either missing or buried so deep that customers assumed they didn't exist.

The result was a product with strong bones and no spine.

§ 02  ·  Why It Was a Problem

In consumer software, bad UX costs engagement. In PAM, it costs the entire security posture.

The core paradox of Privileged Access Management: the harder it is to use, the less secure it makes you. Admins under deadline pressure don't file tickets when a workflow is broken, they route around it. A DevOps engineer who can't get JIT access in under a minute will just use their standing admin credentials. A security admin who can't set up rotation policies without a workshop will skip the policy and rotate credentials manually. Every workaround becomes a shadow IT vulnerability. Every friction point becomes an audit finding.

At the business layer, the pattern was equally painful. Enterprise deals stalled in POC because admins couldn't set up meaningful workflows inside a trial window. Support fielded repetitive tickets on setup steps that should have been self serve. Sales engineers got pulled into implementation calls that ate margin. Compliance customers failed audit prep because the product didn't produce audit ready evidence out of the box.

The product wasn't failing because it lacked features. It was failing because the features didn't hold together as a system.

"If security feels like friction, teams route around it — and the system fails the moment it's needed."
§ 03  ·  How I Solved It

I approached this as a systems problem, not a screens problem. Three phases.

Phase 1  ·  Diagnose before designing

Before touching a single wireframe, I ran a scenario driven UX audit across the existing product. Not happy path click throughs. Real operational scenarios: onboarding a new admin on day one, break glass elevation during a Sev 1, credential rotation across 200+ accounts, offboarding a departing engineer, walking an auditor through a 90 day activity log.

Ten scenarios. Four personas: Security Admins, IT Operations, DevOps and SysAdmins, Compliance Officers. 140+ usability issues surfaced.

But 140 issues isn't a fix list, it's a symptom list. I clustered them into four systemic root causes:

Root cause 01
Fragmented information architecture

14 flat nav items, no task based grouping. Admins had to know the product's internal object model to find anything.

Root cause 02
Broken domain terminology

Object names didn't match the mental model of any of the four personas. Every new admin spent their first week translating the interface before they could use it.

Root cause 03
No workflow continuity

The product was a sum of features, not a system of workflows. Critical paths like JIT, approvals, break glass either didn't exist or required jumping between disconnected sections.

Root cause 04
Silent failure states

Errors weren't surfaced. Discovery surfaced data but didn't guide action. Admins couldn't tell what broke or what to do next.

These four causes — not the 140 surface issues — drove every subsequent decision.

Phase 2  ·  A prioritization framework that ended the debate

The engineering team had been stuck in framework debate for months. Kano models, MoSCoW, RICE. Every exercise produced a different answer and no forward motion.

I collapsed the whole thing to one binary question: does this strengthen a deal breaker capability, or doesn't it?

Deal breakers were the five capabilities that determine whether an enterprise buyer will even consider a PAM product: Vault, Rotation and Reconciliation; Discovery and Inventory; Session Proxying, Recording and OCR; Access Governance (RBAC and JIT); Integrations and Auditability. Anything strengthening these got prioritized. Anything that didn't got deferred, regardless of who asked for it.

Two days of alignment. A meaningful portion of the v1 backlog cut without a single status meeting.

Phase 3  ·  Redesign the system, not the screens

With direction locked, I rebuilt the product around three architectural decisions:

01
Experience architecture over feature list

The old product organized itself by internal object type: Users, Groups, Certificates, Resources, Policies. The redesign organizes itself by admin intent: Overview, Setup, Access Control, Security Ops, Identity Config. A new admin can now find break glass because it lives where break glass belongs, not scattered across three unrelated sections.

02
Workflow continuity within the resource

Instead of forcing admins to piece a workflow together across separate pages, I built the resource detail as a tabbed workspace. Overview, Credentials, Policy, Access, Sessions, Audit trail. All scoped to the resource they're working on. The admin never loses context. Reconciliation credentials — which used to be an undocumented concept — now sit inline in the credentials tab with a plain language explanation of what they do.

03
A six-stage onboarding model

I redesigned admin onboarding as six discrete stages: Discovery, Inventory, Policy, Roles, Provisioning, Access. Each stage produces a verifiable checkpoint. No more setup wizards where you can't tell if you've done it right. Every stage gives the admin evidence they've actually configured the thing they meant to configure.

The new resource detail page. One resource, one context, all the admin's tools in one workspace.

§ 04  ·  Outcome

The v1 shipped as a coherent platform, not a feature list.

$800K
in PAM sales unlocked post release. The redesign directly enabled enterprise deals that had previously stalled at POC, with capability gaps closed and admin onboarding compressed to trial window timelines.

Product outcomes

Workflow continuity

Admins can now complete critical workflows — onboarding, break glass, credential rotation, access allocation, audit review — without leaving the resource they're working on.

Deal-breaker capabilities

The five deal-breaker capabilities that were previously missing or buried now anchor the product's core experience.

Inline error resolution

Error states and reconciliation logic surface inline instead of hiding in documentation. Admins know what broke and exactly how to fix it.

Architecture-level compliance

The product aligns to Zero Trust, SOC 2, and ISO principles by architecture, not by disclaimer.

Team outcomes

Engineering had a single prioritization filter that resolved scope debates in minutes instead of quarters. Design churn dropped significantly by moving decisions from "which screen?" to "which system?" early in the process. PMs, engineers, and design shared a common language — deal breakers, experiences, capabilities — that cut review cycles down to what actually mattered.

§ 05  ·  What I'd Take into the Next One

Three things I'd carry forward.

Audit against scenarios, not screens

Happy path heuristic reviews miss the failures that matter. Audit against what the user is actually trying to do — onboard, break glass, offboard, rotate, prep an audit — and the real friction surfaces immediately. The 10-scenario framework found root causes that a standard heuristic review would have missed entirely.

Prioritization is a communication problem, not a math problem

No framework fixes a stakeholder disagreement. What fixes it is a single, binary question everyone can answer the same way. If your prioritization exercise needs a workshop to interpret, it's the wrong exercise. The deal-breaker filter worked because it reduced a complex decision to one question, not because it was a sophisticated model.

In security products, usability is a security control

Every friction point pushes a user toward a workaround. Every workaround is a vulnerability. Design decisions in PAM aren't cosmetic — they're part of the threat model. The ROI of good UX in this space isn't just NPS. It's measured in reduced shadow IT, cleaner audit trails, and breaches that don't happen.
