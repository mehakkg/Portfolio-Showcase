01 · THE PROBLEM

The opportunity was obvious. The product was not.

Five star hotels prepare extensive spreads to protect their hospitality standard, and a meaningful share of that food goes unsold. At the same time, consumers want hotel quality food without paying hotel prices.

salt peppr · brief
THE OPPORTUNITY · OBVIOUS
Hotels have unsold food from every spread
Consumers want hotel food, not hotel prices
THE PRODUCT · NOT OBVIOUS
Not another delivery app
Supply is unpredictable
The product is perishable
The brand has to survive the word “surplus”

02 · WHY IT WAS HARD

Three problems sat on top of each other.

I owned the product design from business concept to interface. The most important part of the role was refusing to treat each interface as a separate design exercise, so I worked each problem through its own lens.

₹The business problem
Hotels had food with real commercial value and no clean way to redistribute it. Every unsold tray was lost revenue and avoidable waste.
MY LENSThe business experience of hotels managing their offerings.
◎The consumer problem
People already order from Swiggy and Zomato out of habit. A new platform doesn’t win on price alone. It has to give someone a reason to change a routine.
MY LENSThe consumer experience of discovering and buying premium surplus food.
⚙The operational problem
Hotel administrators, delivery partners and Salt Peppr’s internal team all touch the same order, but each needs different information, actions and access.
MY LENSThe operational experience of administrators and delivery partners keeping it running.
solving any one of them in isolation would have broken the other two
THE QUESTION I FRAMED FOR THE PROJECTHow might we make surplus food commercially valuable for hotels, desirable for consumers and manageable for the people running it, inside one coherent product?

03 · DECISION 01 · MARKETPLACE

Design the marketplace before designing the screens.

Salt Peppr is a marketplace with four sides, and every side depends on the others. I mapped who interacts with the system and what each of them needs from it.

HOW AN ORDER MOVES BETWEEN THE FOUR SIDES
  1. HOTEL PARTNERLists surplus foodNeeds to manage surplus food and the business operations around it
  2. CONSUMERDiscovers and ordersNeeds to discover and purchase quality surplus food
  3. HOTEL PARTNERPrepares and hands over
  4. DELIVERY PARTNERPicks up and deliversNeeds to access and fulfil assigned deliveries
  5. CONSUMERReceives and rates
SUPER ADMINSacross every step
IFa hotel cannot list food easily, the consumer opens an empty app.
IFa delivery partner lacks clear information, the premium experience ends at a late doorstep.
DECISIONBuild connected experiences around distinct jobs, instead of one generic interface with everything switched on. That choice shaped the information architecture, navigation, permissions and every workflow that followed.
Next: Decision 02 · Positioning ↓
04 · DECISION 02 · POSITIONING

Sell the opportunity, not the leftovers.

The biggest risk was perception. If consumers read “surplus” as “leftover”, no price point would save the business. The brand had to say two things at once: the food comes from premium hospitality, and it costs less because it would otherwise go unused.

I wanted ordering to feel like discovering an opportunity, not settling for what was left.

DECISIONPosition the experience around premium food discovery and value, with sustainability as a strong supporting reason to buy, not the only selling point. Purpose earns attention, but quality and value change a daily habit.

05 · DECISION 03 · WORKSPACES

One platform, role-specific workspaces.

A hotel administrator, a delivery partner and a super administrator share the same order but not the same priorities. For each role, I defined the information they need, the actions they take and what they should never have to see.

DECISIONSeparate workflows and information by responsibility, while keeping one shared design language. The separation reduced ambiguity about who does what. The shared language kept it one product, maintainable as roles grew.

06 · DECISION 04 · DEVICES

Design for the device each user actually lives on.

Consumers interact on mobile, in short moments. Operational work is information dense and happens on web. Treating both the same would have failed both.

MOBILEConsumersShort moments, on the phone.
WEBOperationsInformation-dense work, on the web.

I kept the visual language consistent across devices, adapted the information hierarchy to each screen size, chose interaction patterns for each context and kept continuity between the consumer and operational sides.

DECISIONTreat mobile, web and admin interfaces as parts of one product ecosystem, not as isolated sets of screens.

08 · WHAT I’D DO DIFFERENTLY

A beautiful consumer experience can’t fix an operating model that doesn’t work.

01Validate the operating model before polishing the interface.Surplus food comes with hard constraints: food safety, availability windows, pricing and logistics. I would spend more time with hotel operations, delivery partners and the internal team before finalising workflows.
02Design trust as a feature, not a finish.With a new food category, freshness and safety are the product. I would test which signals, such as preparation time, source hotel and availability, actually earn consumer confidence.
03Build for scale from day one.Reusable components, role-based permissions and responsive behaviour would make it far easier to onboard new hotels, cities and workflows.
04Define success before shipping.Agree metrics up front: consumer conversion, repeat purchase, hotel activation, fulfilment rate, food redistributed instead of wasted, and revenue from surplus.
