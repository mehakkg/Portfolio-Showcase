01 · THE SHORT VERSION

Premium hotels cook for abundance, not for exact demand.

Premium hotels cook for abundance, not for exact demand, so good food goes unused after every service. Salt Peppr set out to sell that surplus to consumers at an accessible price.

My job was to turn that idea into a working product for four very different users: the consumer buying the food, the hotel listing it, the delivery partner moving it and the internal team running the marketplace.

Two design bets held it together.

BET 01 · CONSUMER SIDEMake the experience feel like access to something premium, not a discount on leftovers.
BET 02 · OPERATIONAL SIDEMake the platform feel like one system with four doors rather than four disconnected tools.
02 · THE PROBLEM

The opportunity was obvious. The product was not.

Five star hotels prepare extensive spreads to protect their hospitality standard, and a meaningful share of that food goes unsold. At the same time, consumers want hotel quality food without paying hotel prices.

The opportunity was obvious. The product was not. Salt Peppr could not be another delivery app. It needed a marketplace where supply is unpredictable, the product is perishable, and the brand has to survive the word “surplus”.

03 · WHY IT WAS HARD

Three problems sat on top of each other.

Three problems sat on top of each other, and solving any one of them in isolation would have broken the other two.

THE BUSINESS PROBLEMHotels had food with real commercial value and no clean way to redistribute it.Every unsold tray was lost revenue and avoidable waste.
THE CONSUMER PROBLEMPeople already order from Swiggy and Zomato out of habit.A new platform does not win on price alone. It has to give someone a reason to change a routine they no longer think about.
THE OPERATIONAL PROBLEMHotel administrators, delivery partners and Salt Peppr’s internal team all touch the same order.But each needs different information, different actions and different levels of access.
THE QUESTION I FRAMED FOR THE PROJECTHow might we make surplus food commercially valuable for hotels, desirable for consumers and manageable for the people running it, inside one coherent product?
04 · MY ROLE

Refusing to treat each interface as a separate design exercise.

I owned the product design from business concept to interface: UX strategy, information architecture, user flows, UI design and the experience across mobile, web and admin surfaces.

The most important part of the role was refusing to treat each interface as a separate design exercise. I worked from three lenses at once:

LENS 01 · CONSUMERThe consumer experience of discovering and buying premium surplus food.
LENS 02 · BUSINESSThe business experience of hotels managing their offerings.
LENS 03 · OPERATIONALThe operational experience of administrators and delivery partners keeping the system running.
05 · DECISION 01

Design the marketplace before designing the screens.

The first decision was a framing one. Salt Peppr is a marketplace with four sides, and every side depends on the others. If a hotel cannot list food easily, the consumer opens an empty app. If a delivery partner lacks clear information, the premium experience ends at a late doorstep.

So before designing any screen, I mapped who interacts with the system and what each of them needs from it.

DECISIONBuild connected experiences around distinct jobs, instead of one generic interface with everything switched on. That choice shaped the information architecture, navigation, permissions and every workflow that followed.
06 · DECISION 02

Sell the opportunity, not the leftovers.

The biggest risk in this product was perception. If consumers read “surplus” as “leftover”, no price point would save the business. The brand had to communicate two truths at the same time: the food comes from premium hospitality, and it costs less because it would otherwise go unused.

I wanted ordering to feel like discovering an opportunity, not settling for what was left.

DECISIONPosition the experience around premium food discovery and value, with sustainability as a strong supporting reason to buy rather than the only selling point. Purpose earns attention, but quality and value are what change a daily ordering habit. That positioning ran through the visual identity, the content hierarchy, how food was presented and the language of the interface.

07 · DECISION 03

One platform, role specific workspaces.

A hotel administrator, a delivery partner and a Salt Peppr super administrator share the same order but not the same priorities. For each role, I defined the information they need, the actions they take and what they should never have to see.

DECISIONSeparate workflows and information by responsibility, while keeping one shared design language across the platform. The separation reduced ambiguity about who does what. The shared language kept it recognisable as one product and kept the design surface maintainable as roles grew.

08 · DECISION 04

Design for the device each user actually lives on.

Consumers interact on mobile, in short moments. Operational work is information dense and happens on web. Treating both the same would have failed both.

I kept the visual language consistent across devices, adapted the information hierarchy to each screen size, chose interaction patterns that suited each context and maintained continuity between the consumer and operational sides.

DECISIONTreat mobile, web and admin interfaces as parts of one product ecosystem, not as isolated sets of screens.

10 · WHAT I’D DO DIFFERENTLY

A beautiful consumer experience cannot compensate for an operating model that does not work.

01Validate the operating model before polishing the interface.Surplus food comes with hard constraints: food safety, availability windows, pricing and logistics. I would spend more time with hotel operations teams, delivery partners and the internal team before finalising workflows. A beautiful consumer experience cannot compensate for an operating model that does not work.
02Design trust as a feature, not a finish.When you introduce a new food category, freshness and safety are the product. I would research how consumers judge quality and test which signals, such as preparation time, source hotel and availability, actually earn their confidence.
03Build for scale from day one.A stronger foundation of reusable components, role based permissions and responsive behaviour would make it far easier to onboard new hotels, new cities and new operational workflows.
04Define success before shipping.I would agree on metrics up front: consumer conversion, repeat purchase rate, hotel partner activation, order fulfilment rate, food redistributed instead of wasted and revenue generated from surplus. That is how design decisions connect to business and sustainability outcomes instead of staying opinions.
