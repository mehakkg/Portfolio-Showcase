01 · THE PROBLEM

Every feature it needed, and nothing tying them together.

Five areas (provisioning, policy, authentication, devices, reporting) were built by different teams at different times. Each one worked. They broke where they met.

03 · MAP THE SYSTEM FIRST

I map the system before I design screens.

habit 01 of 4

Before opening Figma, I mapped what depends on what. The team had argued about build order for a month. The map settled it in one meeting: the policy engine had to be right first, because everything else feeds it or reports on it.

FIG. 2 · THE DEPENDENCY MAP
PROVISIONINGdecides who exists
FEEDS IT ↓
GROUPSdecide how they’re organised
FEEDS IT ↓
POLICY ENGINEhad to be right firstreads both at the moment of access
REPORTS ON IT ↓
REPORTINGproves it happened
Reporting reads groups too.

The map also answered the auditor. Reporting can only explain what the engine wrote down when it decided. So every access decision now records which policy version matched, which groups applied and which conditions passed. An auditor asking about June sees the rule as it stood in June.

04 · FIX WHERE IT STARTS

I fix problems where they start, not where they show up.

habit 02 of 4

My first idea for the two-groups problem was a conflict dialog: show both policies, let the admin pick. But by the time that dialog appears, someone is already locked out.

MY FIRST INSTINCTPOINT OF PAIN
1Admin writes rules
2Two policies collide
3Conflict dialog: pick one
too late: someone’s already locked out
WHAT SHIPPEDPOINT OF FAILURE
1Choose the groups a rule targets
2Then write conditions
3No collision to resolve
the system knows who a rule covers before any condition exists

So I moved the choice to the start. You pick which groups a rule covers before you write a single condition. The conflict can’t happen, so there’s nothing to resolve.

FIG. 3 · THE POLICY BUILDERWho the rule covers comes first.

Same habit, applied to sign-in. A warning icon on SMS is a patch, and admins learn to ignore warnings. Instead, the fifteen methods sit in three tiers: phishing-resistant, strong and fallback. Security keys lead. SMS sits at the bottom, labelled for what it is. Admin accounts can’t drop below the top tier. You can still pick SMS. You just can’t pick it without knowing what you’re giving up.

05 · APPROVED IS STILL OPEN

I treat approved decisions as still open.

06 · THE RIGHT PROBLEM

I check whether I’m solving the right problem.

habit 04 of 4

After twelve interviews, I designed a fourteen-question onboarding form. People abandoned it. I cut it to eight. Still wrong. Five.

FIG. 4 · QUESTIONS IN THE ONBOARDING FORM, ONE SQUARE EACH
ROUND 114People abandoned it.
ROUND 28Still wrong.
ROUND 35Back to my own notes.
SHIPPED3Produces a number: 2 of 7.
WHAT I HAD MISSEDI’d put a questionnaire in front of setup. What they needed was the setup itself, with a finish line.Then I went back to my notes and found what I’d been skimming past: admins had no idea what “finished” looked like.

What shipped is three questions: where your users live today, what you want to protect first, and how people sign in now. The answers pick seven steps, in the order they depend on each other, from connecting your directory to enforcing your first policy.

A checklist counts them: 2 of 7. Seven is what done means.

07 · HOW I KNOW IT WORKED

The best evidence is a disaster that never happened.

We caught the lockout before a line of code was written. If it had shipped, 90% of users would have hit two policies with no way to choose between them. Fixing that would have meant an 8-week migration, by Architecture’s estimate. Instead there was no migration, no affected customers, and nothing to point at.

From the outside, a good architectural call looks like nothing.
