01 · THE STAKES

When a machine checks your work first, every screen is a contract.

The platform scores against a rubric, and the instructor keeps final authority on every grade.

THE ONE HARD RULENo Evidence = No CreditIf the system can’t read a page, that page earns nothing.

The student module is the first and most frequent touchpoint. If it feels cold or punishing, students assume the whole platform is. My job was to make the gate feel like guidance without weakening it.

Three business problems. Every decision had to move one.

01Instructor timeBroken submissions land on faculty desks.
Every dead Drive link, unreadable scan or missing artifact that reaches an instructor is triage the platform promised to remove. Instructor time saved is the renewal metric.
02Dispute liabilityCorrect grades that feel unfair get disputed.
A student who loses marks on a scan issue they never understood will contest the grade. Without a logged record of the warning, the institution has no defence.
03Student trustAI grading is already feared.
In Indian higher education, grades decide careers and word of mouth decides adoption. “A machine grades me with no human in the loop” loses.

02 · THE REAL PROBLEM

Students weren’t failing their work. They were failing a model nobody showed them.

WHAT STUDENTS BELIEVE
Upload→Submit→Done
WHAT THE SYSTEM REQUIRES
Complete→Accessible→Evaluable→Graded
the distance between the two is where marks get lost, for reasons that have nothing to do with the work

Four user problems, each breaking the flow at a different moment.

UP1 · AWARENESS“Submission-ready” is never defined.Gaps show up only after the system rejects the work, usually near the deadline.
UP2 · FORMAT & OCRThe work exists but can’t be read.Permission-locked links, low-quality scans and handwriting all leave the system with no evidence.
UP3 · INTERPRETATIONWarnings and blockers look alike.Anxious students abandon valid work. Rushed students click through risks they don’t understand.
UP4 · RECOVERYFixing means starting over.No preserved work, no targeted fix and no deadline context, so momentum breaks under pressure.
THE REFRAMESolved before upload, not after rejection.Most platforms treat this as an error-message problem and fix it after the fact. I treated it as a mental-model problem that had to be solved before upload.

04 · PRINCIPLES

Four principles, and one rule that overrides them.

Filters, not values on a wall. Every screen went through them in daily critique.

01Error prevention is the spine.Every screen before validation does work, so validation isn’t where students first learn what “complete” means.
CATCH AT VALIDATIONPREVENT BEFORE UPLOAD
02Surface minimalism.Under deadline, students react rather than scan. Hide whatever isn’t load-bearing for the decision on screen.
EVERYTHING, IN CASEONLY LOAD-BEARING

ALL DETAIL UP FRONTOUTCOME FIRST

06 · THE HARDEST DECISION

The dangerous state was the warning, not the failure.

A blocker tells you what to do. “Ready with warnings” asks a stressed student to make an informed trade-off at 11 pm. Two of my personas fail it in opposite directions.

RSRiyaREADS IT AS A BLOCKERAbandons a valid submission and loses marks she never needed to lose.
AMArjunREADS IT AS NOISEClicks through, a criterion scores zero, and he disputes a correct grade.

A generic “I understand” checkbox fails both of them and gives the institution nothing to stand on. So I specified the screen in a fixed order of visual priority:

1State the outcome as a headline,not a status pill in a corner.
2Name the criterion and consequence in plain language.“Page 4 may not be readable, so Criterion 3 may get no evidence credit,” not “OCR confidence below threshold.”
3Give exactly two actions, with the button text carrying the commitment.“Re-upload a clearer page 4” or “Submit anyway. I accept Criterion 3 may score zero.”
4Route “submit anyway” through criterion-by-criterion consent,logged and timestamped server-side, then a reconsideration window before the submission locks.

07 · COURSE CORRECTION 01

The founder told us we’d optimised for the wrong customer.

I ran the first demo as Riya’s journey, then broke it with Marcus, Arjun and Karan. A feature tour gets polite nods. A persona arc gets redirection, and we got three.

FOUNDER FEEDBACK 01“Student load went down. Faculty load might go up.”WHAT IT MEANTThe buyer is the institution, and it buys on faculty time. Five artifacts per student is five things to open.WHAT I CHANGEDI added a single-document model next to multi-artifact. T1 detects links, handwriting and images inside one PDF, and the instructor defines the submission shape before submissions open.
FOUNDER FEEDBACK 02“Real-time AI on every check will bankrupt you.”WHAT IT MEANTCompute cost is a product constraint, not an engineering footnote.WHAT I CHANGEDI split validation into T1/T2 real-time and T3 deferred. The Validating screen tells students when results will arrive instead of making them watch a spinner.
FOUNDER FEEDBACK 03“Pre-submission needs incentives.”WHAT IT MEANTEarly submission is what makes the AI feedback loop valuable.WHAT I CHANGEDI tied analysis depth to timing. Early submitters get full rubric analysis on cheap batch compute. Final-hour submitters get the lighter format and completeness pass.

Roughly a third of the first prototype was invalidated. The four principles held, so we changed surfaces, not philosophy.

30–50%PROJECTED COMPUTE SAVING ON LATE SUBMISSIONSThe alignment I’m proudest of: submitting early gets the student richer feedback and costs the platform less.

09 · BEHAVIOURAL TIERING

Tier by behaviour, never by grade, and never show the bottom tier its label.

Grades measure outcome. Behaviour measures pattern. A student at 65 with strong engagement is a better signal than one at 85 who rush-submits every time.

THE COMPOSITE SCORE · FOUR AXES
Readiness+Autonomy+Iteration Quality+Engagement Depth
Each tier maps to a Self-Determination Theory motivation model. Three tiers, not five: three is legible, five is a spreadsheet.
EstablishedEST. 15–20% OF STUDENTSAdditive unlocks such as stretch criteria, top-percentile samples and trajectory views. More density, same product.
BuildingEST. 55–65%Coaching tuned to the student’s weakest axis, with “Last time you lost marks on…” reminders. The tier that proves the adaptive logic works.
EmergingEST. 15–25% · NO LABEL SHOWNNo tier label, no comparative data, one plain-language fix at a time. Asymmetric disclosure protects these students from labelling harm.
Tiers are additive, not segregated. Shares are planning estimates from the tiering framework, not measured data.
THE DEMO CALLI made the Building tier the spine of the final demo, with Established shown as unlocks and Emerging as a 90-second contrast. About 18 screens instead of 39, with no loss in narrative coverage.

10 · RUNNING THE TEAM

How six designers shipped four weeks without losing a week.

01Strands, not consensus.Primary owners were set in week one. When we flattened ownership in week two to absorb the single-document scope, we lost a day to context switching. I restored the strands and the pace came back.
02Daily critique, not weekly.A short critique after every screen iteration. A weekly review would have produced two big rework cycles. Daily critique produced around fifteen small ones that compounded.
03Persona arcs, not feature tours.The founder got a character to push back on rather than a screen to nitpick. That’s why his feedback came as redirection instead of acknowledgement.
04Decision matrices, not open questions.Calls only the founder could make went to him as options, with the design risk of each spelled out. Open questions get deferred. Matrices get decided.

12 · WHAT I’D DO DIFFERENTLY

Read the buyer’s metric as a design constraint from day one.

REFLECTIONThe brief listed instructor time saved as a business metric, and I treated it as a measurement. It was really a constraint, and missing that cost us a third of the week-one prototype.When a founder talks about who pays, that’s design input.
